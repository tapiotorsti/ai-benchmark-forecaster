# Methodology

[Suomeksi](metodologia.md) · **English**

This page describes how AI Benchmark Aggregator & Forecaster turns benchmark results into a common capability scale and forecasts, and what the results do **not** tell you. Formula details are in the [technical specification](../ai_benchmark_aggregator_forecaster_tekninen_m_rittely.md) (Finnish). Design choices that deviate from the specification (gaps A1–A37) and their rationale are in the [implementation plan](../TOTEUTUSSUUNNITELMA.md) (Finnish).

## 1. Data

- **Benchmark results** come from Epoch AI's open dataset (CC BY 4.0). It covers 61 benchmarks in five domains: general, math, coding, reasoning and agentic. The unit is a *model group*, for example "Claude 3.5 Sonnet (October 2024)". Version identifiers are treated as aliases.
- **Several results for the same model:** within a source, the best result across versions is used for each model and benchmark. If there are several sources, their results are averaged.
- **Prices** come from the price history `model_prices`. Each price has a start date. The sources are simonw/llm-prices, hand-checked launch prices and OpenRouter's current prices. If a model has no price history, OpenRouter's current price is assumed to have applied since release. This may underestimate older prices.
- **Price blending:** the blended price is (3 × input + output) / 4 USD per million tokens.
- **List price, not paid price (decision 2026-11-16):** prices are the cheapest published list prices. This is a consistent definition that can be collected automatically ("the cheapest available price"). On OpenRouter, however, a request can be routed to a more expensive provider, so the average price actually paid can be several times the list price. Cost forecasts therefore describe the best available price, not a typical one.

## 2. Capability scale (continuous 2PL IRT)

Each result is scaled to [0, 1] and clipped to [0.001, 0.999]. For model *i* and benchmark *j*, the expected result is

  ŷ_ij = c_j + (1 − c_j) · σ(a_j (θ_i − b_j))

- **θ_i** is the model's capability, **b_j** the benchmark's difficulty and **a_j = softplus(ã_j)** its discrimination.
- **c_j** is the guessing floor. It is not learned but read from the benchmark metadata (random-guess score divided by the ceiling).
- **Fitting:** parameters are fitted with weighted binary cross-entropy, with L2 priors on θ and b and a prior on ln a. Optimisation uses L-BFGS in PyTorch.
- **Anchoring:** the scale is fixed by two models, GPT-4 (June 2023) = 0 and GPT-5 = 3.5. The anchors set the scale's zero point and unit, so θ values from different runs are on the same scale. Other models' θ can still change between runs as the data changes.
- **Standard error:** the standard error of θ is an HC3 sandwich estimate. In a synthetic Monte Carlo validation, the coverage of the 80% interval was 0.82.
- **Domains:** domain θ is estimated with the benchmark parameters of the overall run held fixed, so domain θ is directly on the same scale.

**Quality control**
- **Pruning:** a model needs at least 3 results and a benchmark at least 5.
- **Outliers:** for every result on benchmarks with at least 12 results, a standardised residual and from it a per-benchmark robust z (MAD) are computed. A result with |z| > 3 is flagged. Only suspiciously *good* results, which may indicate contamination, are removed from the fit, and at most one per model. Poor results are not removed, because they can reflect a genuine weakness.
- **Checks:** scale stability is checked by leaving out one benchmark at a time. The scale is also compared with Epoch's own ECI index, with a target Spearman correlation > 0.9. ECI is Epoch's IRT-type model of largely the same data, so this comparison checks reproducibility, not independent validity.
- **Predictive validity (A36, `forecaster validate`, [validointi.md](validointi.md)):** the scale is tested by predicting results the fit did not see. 2026-11-25:
  - *Held-out results* (5-fold cross-validation, 3,201 observations, 300 models, 60 benchmarks): the IRT model's RMSE was 0.089 on the normalised scale (0.086 as the mean of per-benchmark RMSEs). The baselines were fitted to the same training observations: a 1PL-type model (same BCE loss and clipping as IRT, but a common discrimination) got 0.114 (0.108) and the benchmark mean 0.233 (0.189). The difference between IRT and 1PL therefore measures the benefit of per-benchmark discrimination (priors, ridge and optimisation differ slightly). IRT was best in all five domains. The mean point prediction matched the observed mean by decile (difference at most about 0.01), but the benchmark mean also comes close on this measure, so it does not prove discrimination on its own. It does not measure prediction intervals. All fits converged.
  - *Cold start for a new benchmark* (one benchmark left out at a time, its parameters fitted to the results of five random models, 20 draws per benchmark): RMSE 0.092 as the median of per-benchmark medians, 0.105 as the mean over all draws and 0.164 as their P90. The mean of the calibration models got 0.196, 0.206 and 0.328 respectively. 2PL beat the mean for 86 % (49/57) of the benchmarks. In 29 % of the draws 2PL was worse than a model where only the difficulty is fitted, probably because two parameters are estimated from five points (hypothesis). Three benchmarks were skipped because fewer than 10 models with known θ had results on them. There are eight exceptions. Six of them (lambada, piqa, triviaqa, deepresearch_bench, cl_bench, cl_bench_life) have little spread in their results (standard deviation of the normalised result about 0.04–0.10), so the mean of the calibration models is probably already close to the best prediction (hypothesis, not verified); four of these also have low discrimination (a < 0.35). Posttrainbench was practically a tie, and spireclimb had only 8 models to evaluate.
  - *Limitations:* the validation is internal to the data. It tells how well one θ per model and two parameters per benchmark explain the observed results, but not that θ measures general capability beyond the data. The hidden results are a random sample of the observed ones, although the real missingness is not random. Outliers, pruning and anchors were decided on the full data. The calibration models in the cold start are drawn from the whole θ range, although new benchmarks are typically run on top models first, so the cold-start result is probably optimistic (hypothesis). No independent source has been compared.

## 3. Capability forecast

1. **Pareto frontier:** the frontier consists of models whose θ exceeds that of every previously released model. Of models released on the same day, only the best can join the frontier. The fit starts on 2023-01-01.
2. **Trend:** the frontier's θ is fitted as a straight line over time. The alternative is a piecewise model with a hinge 12 months before the forecast date. The piecewise model is chosen when the hinge coefficient has p < 0.05.
3. **Bootstrap:** in each round, θ is drawn from N(θ̂, SE²), and the drawn θ decides which models belong to the frontier. Frontier points are resampled with replacement, the trend is fitted to the models' *original* θ̂ values, and the model (linear or piecewise) is chosen again. Measurement error is not fed into the fit a second time, because it already shows in the residuals. The prediction interval thus includes uncertainty about the frontier's composition, the trend, and whether progress is linear or accelerating.
   - **Fit window (A28):** in each round, the fit starts at random (with equal probabilities) either on 2023-01-01 or 18, 24 or 36 months before the forecast date. A window is dropped if it would start before 2023-01-01 or its frontier has fewer than 5 points, so older backtest origins have fewer options. Short windows skip the hinge test, unless the full-period model is piecewise: then windows that span the hinge are tested too, so that they do not mix the old and new pace. Intervals always widen somewhat (synthetically, with a steady pace, coverage went from 0.82 to 0.87, above the 0.80 target) and more when the recent and long-run pace differ. The median also moves towards the recent pace.
   - **Reported pace (A29):** the median slope of the bootstrap distribution, i.e. the same distribution the horizon forecasts come from. The horizon-specific cost floor (A26) uses the same value, and in the backtest it is computed only from data released by the origin date. The current slope of the full-period fit is shown as a diagnostic (2026-11-19: median 1.84 θ/yr, full-period linear fit 1.68 θ/yr).
4. **Reported intervals:**
   - The **trend** interval describes the uncertainty of the trend line.
   - The **prediction** interval describes the spread of a single future frontier model.
5. **Backtest:** the model is fitted only with data released by the origin date, and the forecast is compared with every frontier point realised after it. There are four origins (6, 12, 18 and 24 months ago), because the errors of one origin share a sign and amount to practically a single observation. The trend is fitted only to models released by the origin, but their θ comes from the current IRT run, which was also fitted with results published later. Epoch evaluates a model a median of 63 days after its release (more than a year for 10 % of models), so this backtest looks into the future.
   - **Point-in-time backtest (A35, `forecaster backtest-vintage`):** the IRT model is refitted for every origin with the same pipeline as in production, but using only the results that were in Epoch's data package (Internet Archive snapshots every 1–1.5 months from 2025-11; May 2026 is missing). The origins are the snapshot dates, so the data is neither stale nor repeated. Scores, release dates, version grouping, benchmark selection and anchors come from the current data, and the method settings were chosen with backtests from this same period, so the set-up is only approximately forward-looking. Result ([backtest_ajankohta.md](backtest_ajankohta.md), 10 origins 2025-11 … 2026-09, as_of 2026-11-24): the prediction interval covered 0.84 of the realised points averaged over origins and 0.89 over all points (target 0.80; the ordinary backtest on the same origins gave 1.00). The mean error (realised − P50) was +0.13 θ and the mean absolute error 0.21 θ. Corrected for the level shift of the scale (sensitivity), coverage was 0.71 over origins and 0.78 over points. The positive error comes from the most recent realised points: for mature points (model released at least 3 months before as_of) coverage was 1.00, the mean error −0.03 θ and the mean absolute error 0.11 θ. The most recent points are also those with longer horizons and from the latest period. One possible explanation (hypothesis, cf. the synthetic experiment of A31) is that the θ of the newest frontier models rests on few results and is biased upwards by selection; the data cannot tell this apart from the horizon length or genuine acceleration. The only firm conclusion is that the ordinary backtest was optimistic (coverage 1.00 vs 0.84). The point-in-time result neither refutes nor confirms the impression from A33 of too-wide intervals: the 72 point evaluations come from only 10 different models (5 mature), so the data cannot tell whether the intervals are slightly narrow, about right or slightly wide.
6. **How earlier forecasts turned out (A30):** each forecast stores its prediction interval month by month for 24 months ahead. Later runs compare stored forecasts (the latest of each ISO week; retroactive runs are skipped) with frontier points released after them, at each release date. Unlike the backtest, this comparison does not refit the model. Tracking started on 2026-11-19, so results only accumulate as new record models are released. Realised θ values come from the current IRT run, so a model's θ can differ from its value at forecast time. **Method versions (A34):** the method was tuned for a long time on the same small data, so it was frozen as version 1 on 2026-11-24. The version is stored with every forecast, and the tracking reports results per version (version 0 = forecasts made before versioning). The method is changed only if the rationale was recorded before the change is evaluated on real data and the change has been validated synthetically. Changes are recorded in the method version table of the [implementation plan](../TOTEUTUSSUUNNITELMA.md).
7. **True-capability forecast (A31):** next to the main forecast, a forecast with the frontier selection bias removed is shown. The bias is estimated with a parametric bootstrap for the same estimator as the main forecast (including fit windows and model selection): θ̂ is taken as true, measurement error N(0, SE²) is added, and the forecast median on the noisy data is compared with the noise-free one using the same random numbers. The mean difference is the bias per horizon; the correction shifts the whole distribution and leaves the interval width unchanged. In a synthetic experiment (`scripts/validate_selection_bias.py`, 100 repetitions) the correction reduced the mean error against true records from −0.27 to −0.03 θ (SE 0.3) and from −0.12 to −0.06 θ (SE 0.15). The remaining error is of the same size as the main forecast's own error against observed records (−0.05…−0.06). The shifted P10–P90 covered true records 0.85 (SE 0.15) and 0.94 (SE 0.3) of the time, so for true capability the interval is somewhat wide. The estimate is approximate: θ̂ already contains noise, so the bias tends to come out slightly too small. With current data (overall forecast 2026-11-23, median frontier θ-SE about 0.17, i.e. closer to the SE 0.15 case) the bias is about 0.15–0.25 θ at the 6–24-month horizons.
8. **Uncertain models excluded:** models with θ-SE > 1 are left out of the forecast. In the 2026-11-16 run there were 21 such models. Nearly all had only 3–5 results, their benchmarks had below-average discrimination, and they sat far below the benchmarks' difficulties. All of these weaken how well θ is determined. None of them had all results at the guessing floor.
9. **Comparison forecast (A32, A33):** the frontier has few points, which makes its slope uncertain. The report shows a comparison forecast next to the main forecast. Its pace is fitted to the three best models of each calendar quarter, and its level is taken from the frontier points. It forecasts the same quantity (a frontier point) and assumes that the upper edge and the frontier grow at the same pace. The assumption is not certain: the level of a quarter's three best models also rises when the number of releases grows. The bootstrap resamples quarters with replacement, because models from the same quarter are not independent. An unfinished quarter is left out of the pace. The comparison uses the same fit windows as the main forecast (A28), but it has no hinge and no small-sample correction, and it is not used in the forecasts. Its purpose is to show whether the methods start to diverge. The script `scripts/compare_top_k.py` evaluates both on the same 9 origins 3–27 months before 2026-11-23 (65 overlapping evaluation points; at the 30-month origin the main forecast failed because the frontier was too small, 4 points):

   | | main forecast | comparison |
   |---|---|---|
   | mean absolute error θ | 0.41 | 0.16 |
   | – origins 3–21 months | 0.13–0.31 | 0.11–0.25 |
   | – origins 24 and 27 months (frontier of 6 and 5 points) | 0.96 and 1.52 | 0.18 and 0.20 |
   | mean error θ | +0.33 | +0.01 |
   | inside the interval, mean over origins (expected ≈ 0.80) | 0.84 | 1.00 |
   | mean interval width θ | 1.47 | 1.17 |

   The difference came mostly from the oldest origins, where the main forecast's trend missed. At the 3–21-month origins the comparison error was slightly smaller (point-weighted about 0.15 vs 0.21; smaller at 6 of 7 origins). Using the single best model (mean absolute error 0.33) instead of three was worse, and the five best (0.17) were almost as good. There are few origins and they overlap, so the result is indicative only.

   **Interval width (A33, `scripts/diagnose_interval_width.py`):** at the 3–21-month origins both intervals covered every point. The main forecast's interval width is dominated by trend uncertainty (trend P10–P90 1.13, prediction 1.42). The trend held at the 3–21-month origins, but at 24–27 months it missed so that the interval was too narrow (coverage 0.57 and 0.00). The main forecast's intervals are therefore not demonstrably too wide. The result fits errors at the same origin being correlated (hypothesis), but the data cannot tell this apart from the interval being too wide at recent origins and too narrow after the break. The mean over origins, 0.84, consists of seven 1.00s, one 0.57 and one 0.00, so it does not show calibration. The comparison interval, by contrast, is too wide relative to its own errors, and its width comes from residual noise: the noise scatter derived from the interval width is about 0.34 (assuming normality and independent trend and noise), while the realised RMSE at the 3–21-month origins was 0.18.

   **The main cause is a single point.** The first frontier point of the fit period is GPT-4 (2023-03, residual +0.97), followed by a plateau until mid-2024 (gpt-4o and claude-3-5-sonnet, residuals about −0.5). GPT-4 is a leverage point: at the 0–15-month origins the frontier's residual scatter is 0.31–0.38 with it (0.34–0.45 with the steeper top-3 slope) and 0.19–0.23 without it, which is almost the realised RMSE of 0.18. The half-yearly means of the absolute residuals in 2025–2026 are 0.03–0.21 (individual residuals −0.29…+0.31). The same point drags down the main forecast's slope at old origins, where the frontier has few points: at 21 months the frontier OLS slope is 1.13 with GPT-4 and 1.75 without it, at 24 months 0.79 and 1.27. This explains part of the main forecast's miss at 24–27 months, so the miss is not independent evidence of random trend uncertainty. That is why the comparison uses fit windows. They narrowed its interval by about 14 % at the 3–21-month origins and about 5 % at 24–27 months, but did not improve accuracy (mean absolute error 0.14 → 0.16), and the interval is still clearly wider than the errors (3–21 months: scatter derived from the interval about 0.41 vs RMSE 0.18). The windows do not remove GPT-4's effect, because about a quarter to a third of the draws are fitted to the full period. At the oldest origins only the 18-month window fits, and at 27 months even that one includes GPT-4. The noise model was not changed, because tuning it on the same few origins would be overfitting. Limitation: the θ values come from the current IRT run, which was also fitted with data released after the origin, so the realised errors are probably slightly optimistic.

   The pace interval is narrower in the comparison (run of 2026-11-24: 1.81–2.04 vs 1.67–2.11 θ/yr), partly because of its structure: it does not include model-structure (hinge) uncertainty. In a synthetic experiment where models from the same quarter are correlated (100 repetitions, ± 0.03–0.04; the setup of the test `test_top_k_slope_interval_coverage_with_quarter_effects`) its coverage was 0.72 without windows and 0.89 with them (target 0.80), so with windows it is somewhat wide, as is the main forecast. On current data the two methods give almost the same P50 (12 months 7.78 vs 7.69), and the comparison prediction interval is as wide or narrower at every horizon (12 months: 0.83 vs 0.94 θ).
10. **Interpretation: the unit of θ, reference benchmarks and the measurability ceiling (A37):** θ is a relative scale fixed by the anchors (for overall capability GPT-4 June 2023 = 0, GPT-5 = 3.5), so "θ per year" does not by itself say what a model can do. A benchmark's logit score is a·(θ − b), so a one-unit rise in θ changes it by a; on a well-discriminating benchmark (a ≈ 1.4, c = 0) a one-unit rise takes the result from, for example, 50 % to about 80 %. The report and dashboard therefore translate the forecast into expected results on reference benchmarks (HLE, Remote Labor Index, FrontierMath Tier 4, WeirdML v3; `reference_benchmarks` in `config/forecast.yaml`) with the IRT formula c + (1 − c)·σ(a(θ − b)) for a model whose θ is the current frontier value or a forecast percentile. The figures are model expectations, not observed results, and the best observed result is shown alongside (it may come from a model weaker than the frontier). Weakly discriminating reference benchmarks (a < 1) are flagged; for example HLE has a = 0.94, and its top models' observed results fall about 9–10 percentage points below the IRT curve, so the HLE translation appears to overestimate at the top (observation 2026-11-26, not tested on other runs). The interval reflects the uncertainty of the θ forecast, not benchmark-specific scatter around the curve or the uncertainty of the benchmark parameters. The translation inherits the assumptions of the IRT model (one dimension, benchmark parameters from the current run). The **measurability ceiling** is the largest b + k/a among benchmarks with discrimination a ≥ 1 and at least 15 models excluding outliers (k = 2: the expected result on the benchmark that sets the ceiling is about 88 % of the way from the guessing floor to the ceiling; decision 2026-11-25). Above it well-measured benchmarks discriminate only weakly, so checking a θ forecast that reaches there with current benchmarks is uncertain: whether it comes true also depends on whether harder benchmarks appear and link to the scale. The ceiling is a convention, not an observed breakpoint: at the ceiling the slope of the expected result is still about 15 percentage points per θ unit (a = 1.39), and the next θ unit raises it by about 9 percentage points (88 % → 97 %), so discrimination weakens gradually. In domain views the ceiling and the reference benchmarks are computed from that domain's own benchmarks only. In the run of 2026-11-25 the ceiling was θ 7.70 (Remote Labor Index, a 1.39, b 6.26, 19 models). The ceiling is unstable: it depends on a single benchmark (next candidate WeirdML v3, θ 6.74), the benchmark's b lies above the θ of every model it has measured (best observed result 41 %), and its own uncertainty is not shown (the review's bootstrap over the benchmark's 19 models with θ held fixed: P10–P90 7.51–8.03, a lower bound on the true uncertainty). The 12-month forecast's P50 is practically at the ceiling: in the run of 2026-11-25 it was just below (7.6966 vs 7.6969) and on 2026-11-26 just above (7.7015), because the horizon is counted from the run date and the P50 rises by about 0.005 θ per day; the 18- and 24-month P50s exceeded it. For example, the expected HLE result was 73 % for the current frontier (best observed 55 %) and 93 % at the 12-month P50, and for Remote Labor Index 42 % (41 %) and 88 %. FrontierMath Tier 4 is already saturated (the current frontier is above its own ceiling, observed results 98–100 %), so it says little about the forecast. The ceiling and the translation do not change the forecast, so they are not part of the method version.

## 4. Cost forecast

For a chosen capability level θ_target, the cheapest model with θ ≥ θ_target and a valid price is found for each month.

- **What is fitted:** the regression ln(price) = α0 − α1·t is fitted to *price records*, i.e. months in which the cheapest price fell, not to repeated monthly values. Repeated values would narrow the interval artificially.
- **Halving time:** T½ = ln 2 / α1. If α1 ≤ 0, the halving time is undefined.
- **Stalled decline:** a piecewise model and a floor test detect whether the decline has stalled.
- **Clipping the forecast:** the forecast is clipped to [price floor, the level's current cheapest price]. The cheapest available price cannot rise.
- **Floor per horizon:** capability rises by β θ per year (the frontier pace). So in h months, the level's capability costs roughly what a model β·h/12 θ weaker costs now. The floor is the cheapest model with level − β·h/12 ≤ θ < level. It is used only if that model is clearly cheaper (below 2/3 of the level's current cheapest price). Otherwise the floor is the cheapest model in the whole market. A nearby model at almost the same price says nothing about how far the price can fall, and would freeze the forecast. Models above the level are not counted, because the level's own cheapest model would then always be the floor.
- **State `at_floor`:** the level is already practically at the market-wide price floor (cheapest price at most 1.5 × the market's cheapest). The forecast assumes the price will not fall below the market's current cheapest, so progress is assumed to show as rising capability at the same price. New cheap models can, however, lower the floor.

## 5. Limitations – read before interpreting

- **Small sample:** the frontier has only about 20 points. Prediction intervals are wide, and P50 is not a prediction but the middle of the distribution.
- **Calibration cannot yet be verified:** in the four-origin backtest, the average share of points inside the prediction interval per origin was 0.93 ± 0.07 (SE), and 0.93 of all evaluated points fell inside (target 0.80; measured 2026-11-18 with the default settings, i.e. small-sample correction A27 and fit windows A28). The figure is sensitive: on 2026-11-17 with the same data it was 0.88 ± 0.12 without fit windows, and 0.75 ± 0.25 without either correction. Moving the origins by one day, or the bootstrap draws, change individual origins' hits (oldest origin 2/6–5/7). Errors from the same origin share a sign, so the effective sample size is at most the number of origins (4), not the number of points. Origins also share evaluation points and training data, so the standard error is probably an underestimate. This backtest, however, uses current θ values and looks into the future. In the point-in-time backtest (A35, item 3.5) coverage was 0.84 (0.89 over all points), close to the target, but it rests on only 10 different models. Tracking of genuinely forward-looking forecasts (A30) started on 2026-11-19, and its results will accumulate over time.
- **Structural change:** the forecast from origin 2024-11 underestimated later progress: the mean error (realised − P50) is about +0.9 θ and coverage 0.71 (5/7), also with fit windows (measured 2026-11-18). The full-period slope was then 0.79 θ/yr. The 2023 frontier has only two points (0.39 → 0.58, uncertain), whereas the 2024 points released by the origin date show a pace of about 1.7 θ/yr (4 points; 2.2 including o1 from December 2024). Fit windows (A28) mitigate such a situation. In a synthetic experiment (acceleration 15 months before the origin), prediction-interval coverage rose from 0.70 to 0.81. At origin 2024-11, however, only the 18-month window is available, because longer ones would start before 2023. The windows were chosen with the same synthetic scenario they are tested on, so the experiment is not an independent validation. In the real backtest, four origins are not enough to tell the methods apart. Windows do not anticipate changes that happen *after* the forecast date.
- **Frontier selection bias:** models whose measurement error happened to be positive end up on the frontier. Observed records are therefore higher than the *true* θ. The main forecast concerns observed records, which future records will be selected into in the same way, and relative to them it is nearly unbiased. Relative to true capability it is biased upwards: in a synthetic experiment (100 repetitions) the forecast overestimated true future records by 0.27 θ with SE 0.3 and by 0.12 θ with SE 0.15. The report and dashboard therefore also show a bias-corrected forecast (A31, see section 3).
- **Small-sample intervals:** in a synthetic Monte Carlo experiment (2026-11-17; true linear trend), the 80% prediction interval covered 0.81 ± 0.02 of future frontier points with a frontier of about 16 points, but only 0.72 ± 0.02 with about 9 points. Intervals are therefore widened automatically when the frontier has fewer than 12 points (A27; 0.80 ± 0.02 in the experiment). The threshold of 12 is a decision (2026-11-17), not a measured breakpoint: coverage has only been measured for frontiers of about 9 and 16 points. The correction currently applies to the two oldest rolling-backtest origins (6 and 9 frontier points) and to small domains, and the report shows when it was applied. **Pace interval (P10–P90):** without fit windows the interval was too narrow: in the same 250-repetition experiment it covered the true value only 0.68–0.73 (± 0.03) of the time, also with the small-sample correction, and the cause was the interval's narrowness, not bias in the point estimate. With fit windows (A28) the picture reversed: with a steady pace the interval covered the true value 0.88 ± 0.02 (frontier of about 12 points) and 0.92 ± 0.02 (about 20 points), so it is somewhat too wide (synthetic Monte Carlo 2026-11-23, 200 repetitions). In the same experiment without windows, coverage was 0.69 / 0.60. Analytical intervals (OLS and HC3) and a block bootstrap fell clearly short of the target in the same experiment (0.48–0.72). The width comes from the fit windows. The windows were chosen because they improve the prediction-interval coverage of future points when the pace changes (A28). The coverage of the pace interval when the pace changes has not been measured.
- **The price floor is a heuristic:** if there are no clearly cheaper models near the level, the floor is the cheapest model in the whole market, even if its capability is below the level. The floor can also be too tight, and the backtest reports if the realised price fell below the trained floor. The clipped backtest error then measures the floor's accuracy, not the trend model, so the report also shows the unclipped error.
- **Contamination:** outlier filtering only detects sharp individual cases. It does not detect broad, uniform contamination.
- **One dimension:** IRT assumes a single capability dimension. Domain runs give a per-domain view but use the same benchmark parameters.
- **Price data licence:** simonw/llm-prices has no licence, and OpenRouter's terms of service prohibit automated collection and redistribution of its data. Price-based figures are therefore not published: the forecast report and dashboard are local only.
